# Flaws in new ad law

**In January, the UK government released a new regulation that bans the advertisement of food and drinks high in fat, salt and sugar on TV before 9 pm and at any time online.** This action is expected to remove up to 7.2 billion calories from UK children's diets each year, according to the government.

However, "this policy is at risk of being a paper tiger," John Barber, director of Nesta, a London-based charity, expressed his concern to The Guardian. After analyzing data from 2004 to 2024, the latest Nesta report estimated that the ban will ultimately affect only 1 percent of annual food and drink advertising spending.

Notably, the regulation doesn't cover ads on outdoor advertising such as billboards, bus stops and cinemas. So firms might shift their spending from restricted TV and online ads to these less-regulated formats. In fact, the country's Food Foundation reported a 28 percent increase in outdoor advertising spending by food companies from 2021 to 2024. **Outdoor ads can trigger food cravings just as effectively as TV ads and they can reach children easily on the school run, noted Nesta.**

Instead, they rely on storytelling, mood or lifestyle imagery to draw in customers, as Beverley O'Hara, a lecturer at Leeds Beckett University in the UK, wrote in The Conversation.

But why are there exemptions? It's because certain industries, including food, have used powerful lobbyists to convince the UK government not to adopt health-improving policies that may affect their business, Chris Whitty, Chief Medical Adviser to the UK government, said in a lecture. Data from the Food Foundation also showed that in late 2024 and early 2025, ministers met with the food industry 10 times more often than with non-governmental organizations. At the same time, the media often label these measures as "nanny state policies", suggesting that the government is interfering too much in people's choices. This can turn public opinion against such policies and make them harder to carry out, Whitty added.

According to O'Hara, to solve diet-related health problems, the UK needs to change its food culture and values. Healthier foods should be more appealing, affordable and easy to access. The new ad ban is a small step, but eating better needs to be much easier.

# One step closer to the moon

Humans may soon set foot on the moon again. On April 1, NASA's Artemis II mission lifted off from Florida, US, sending four astronauts aboard the Orion spacecraft on the first crewed flight around the moon in more than 50 years. "Humanity's next great voyage begins," NASA spokesperson Derrol Nail said during the agency's launch webcast.

Artemis II is NASA's first crewed mission under its Artemis lunar exploration program, following the uncrewed Artemis I mission in November 2022. Artemis III, planned for 2027, will test systems in low Earth orbit before the lunar landing of Artemis IV in 2028.

The crew includes NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, along with Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen. Their journey around the moon was planned to last about 10 days. And they were expected to practice a wide range of skills needed for deep space exploration, including testing Orion's life-support systems and practicing operations essential for future lunar missions, according to China Daily.

Humans' interest in the moon never seems to fade, as lunar exploration shows a country's economic and technological strength.

**Back in 1969, during the Cold War, US astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin of the Apollo 11 mission became the first humans to walk on the moon.** Nearly 60 years later, the Artemis program is more than just a return to the lunar surface, according to Space. Com. Its goal is to establish a sustainable human presence on the moon and use its resources to support future exploration of Mars and more distant destinations, as noted by the science news website.

# Teaching not for clicks

**A growing number of educators across China are pointing cameras at students to record classroom moments and campus life for social media posts.** This trend raises serious questions: Is this practice legal? Is it ethical from a professional point of view? And, perhaps most critically, what are the consequences for the children being filmed?

**Under China's Personal Information Protection Law, facial data and other biometric information of minors under the age of 14 are considered "sensitive personal information".** Collecting, keeping and sharing such data requires a guardian's permission.

Furthermore, the principle of data minimization, written in the Law on the Protection of Minors, says that only the minimum personal information necessary for a specific purpose should be processed. This principle is clearly violated when a teacher films an entire classroom to capture a single“funny moment”. Even for adult students, the legal barriers remain high. The Civil Code clearly states that no individual may produce, use or publicly share another person's image without permission.

Beyond the complex legal questions lies an even more fundamental ethical dilemma. Producing high-quality short videos is time-consuming. The energy devoted to chasing "likes" and followers is energy taken from lesson planning and professional development.

When a teacher's online persona becomes their primary focus, students are gradually transformed into props in the teacher's personal reality show. The risks to students should not be dismissed. Once a child's image is uploaded to a public platform, control is lost. Their images can be used for AI manipulation or even fraud.

To address this phenomenon, educational authorities must establish clear regulations defining the boundary between "teaching scenes" and "private scenes". Meanwhile, schools should ban filming that affects the educational order or commercializes students.

And perhaps most importantly, video platforms must act as "gatekeepers" of the public sphere. They should carefully check teachers' identities, strictly review videos involving children, and obtain written permission from parents before posting such videos online. The classroom is a space for educating young minds, not a place for chasing clicks. **It is time for teachers to put the cameras down**, respect students' privacy and dignity, and let education go back to basics.

# Prepared for AI future

Jules White used to believe his 11-year-old son needed to know how to code to be successful. Now, the computer science professor says it's more important for James to learn a more useful skill: how to use AI chatbots.

Since OpenAI released ChatGPT in 2022, White has been teaching James about generative AI. He began by demonstrating to James how ChatGPT can create games using photos of toys. Later, White showed him AI's mistakes by having his son check ChatGPT-generated world record claims against accurate information from the Guinness Book of World Records. Over two years, James has learned how to use AI for everyday activities, like making study materials and finding the cost of shoes without a price tag.

"**My goal isn't to make him a generative AI wizard**," White said. "It's to give him a foundation for using AI to be creative, explore perspectives and improve his learning."

White is part of a growing number of parents teaching their young children how to use AI chatbots, so they are prepared to use the tools responsibly as personal assistants for school, work and life when they're older.

Parents are curious about how generative AI will affect their kids' personal growth. Nearly a third of parents say AI tools such as ChatGPT should be banned in schools to avoid cheating, according to a 2023 Ipsos poll, as a growing number of young students rely on chatbots to do their homework. Despite limited research on AI's impact on kids' critical thinking skills, Unicef and other child health organizations are questioning how a dependency on chatbots for classwork can affect cognitive development. AI's rapid evolution could also make it harder for less tech-savvy parents to intervene when children misuse it.

While OpenAI and Google's terms of service say their AI models are for users 13 and older, some parents are bending the rules and introducing AI to their young kids anyway. But they do so with caution. These parents hope to help their children see AI as an imperfect tool that can expand rather than restrict human abilities.

"This is going to take over everything," White said. "I want him to be prepared." White also emphasizes responsible AI use. Without guidance, children might use ChatGPT improperly, which may lead to "intellectual laziness".
